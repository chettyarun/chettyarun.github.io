Most companies buy AI tools and call it transformation.

That is the wrong order.

Access matters. Models matter. Agents matter. But none of that sticks if the culture still treats AI as a side project for a few engineers and a slide in the all-hands.

If you want an AI-native org, the hard part is not the stack. The hard part is getting thousands of people to build with it in their real jobs — sales, finance, CS, legal, design, HR — until it stops feeling like a program and starts feeling like how work gets done.

That is culture change.

At Razorpay we have been running at this for the better part of a year. In August we ran Day0 (#FeelTheAGI), our company hackathon. More than 1,500 people registered. Hundreds of teams shipped working prototypes. Sales, finance, CS, legal, engineering, design — same bar for everyone.

Day0 looked like an event. It was the exam after a year of class.

Here is how we built the culture that made that possible.

## 1. Give everyone access first

Culture does not start with a manifesto. It starts with whether people can actually use the thing.

We did not wait for every team to invent their own setup. Everyone got open-weight models and a custom harness we call Razorpay Cowork. The MCPs and connectors each function needed were built centrally. Sales needed CRM on day one — it was already plugged in. A new joiner opened their laptop and the harness was waiting.

Skills were maintained centrally too. If one team built something useful, everybody gained from it. One person's agent became the org's agent.

That sounds operational. It is cultural. You cannot ask people to become AI builders and then make them file a ticket to try.

## 2. Teach agentic work on their actual jobs

Most business teams had never done agentic work. Pointing them at ChatGPT and saying "go" does almost nothing.

So we went team by team. Hands-on. Their tools. Their mess.

For Talent Acquisition we showed agents that find and filter candidates. For sales we showed multiple connectors working together — Tableau, the data lake, CRM, support desk — in one flow that made the job faster.

The point was not a generic AI masterclass. The point was: this is how *your* work changes.

## 3. Make building visible — Show & Tell

If people only build in private, culture never spreads.

We ran weekly Show & Tell sessions where anyone could present what they had built. One week it was a MoM generator from sales. The next it was a PRD reviewer from a PM. Employee-led. Practical. No stage production required.

Local teams ran their own show-and-tells too. Each team got a place to stash what they built, a day in the week to showcase it, and a Slack channel to show off work.

Champions showed up on their own. People started teaching each other. Founders started monthly sessions highlighting interesting builds they had seen.

That is the moment it stopped feeling like a central program and started feeling like the org just… builds.

I wrote about an earlier version of this journey [on X](https://x.com/ChettyArun/status/1962479435329986791). The pattern has only gotten stronger since.

## 4. Leaders go first

I have written before about [hands-on leadership](https://chettyarun.com/posts/handson-leadership/). AI makes that non-negotiable.

You cannot ask your org to build with AI while leaders only review decks about AI.

Last year we ran a leadership AI hackathon. Every leader from Finance to HR built something. No spectators. [Harshil wrote about it](https://x.com/harshilmathur/status/1936010231236194762).

This year at Day0 we did it again. Leaders went first. Then they mentored org teams — sat with people before the event, ran brainstorming sessions for folks who had no idea yet, posted problem statements and bounties on the portal, and stayed on the floor for the three build days.

Leaders who have felt the tools can sponsor what their teams invent. Leaders who have not stay in slide mode.

## 5. Hackathons as proof, not theater

We have always believed hackathons should not be engineering-only. I wrote about that back in 2023 in [Hackathons for business teams](https://chettyarun.com/posts/Hackathon-for-business-teams/). Day0 was that idea at full scale.

A few things that mattered:

- Registration was an idea doc, not a form. An AI agent scored ideas and left comments so teams could refine before Day 1.
- Working prototypes only. Code plus a short pitch video. No slideware.
- AI judging first, then human rounds. Same bar across functions.
- Leadership bounties on top of the main prize pool — weird, fun prizes from leaders' own budgets — so more people had a reason to build even if they were not chasing the overall win.

The event was the last step of enablement, not the first. That is why 1,500 people could show up and actually ship.

## 6. Measure to guide, not to dunk

Once adoption was real, we needed a shared language for where people stood.

We use a simple L1 to L5 scale: Aware → Exploring → Practising → Amplifying → Redefining.

Not to judge people. To see who is flying and who still needs a nudge — and to change training by cohort instead of running one generic workshop for 3,000 humans.

You cannot manage what you refuse to look at. You also cannot build trust if "measurement" feels like a gotcha. Guide, do not dunk.

## What this is really about

AI culture is not free snacks and a Slack emoji.

It is:

1. Access for everyone, not a pilot for a few
2. Enablement on real jobs, not chatbot tourism
3. Visible building — Show & Tell, champions, peer teaching
4. Leaders who build first
5. Hackathons that prove the org can ship
6. Measurement that redirects energy instead of shaming people

Tools will keep changing. Models will keep getting cheaper. The orgs that win will be the ones whose people already know how to build when the next model drops.

If you are trying to make your company AI-native, start with culture. The stack follows.
